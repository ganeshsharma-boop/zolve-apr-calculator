The interest you avoid is the real saving. Look at the rupees saved, not the APR. Fewer months of repayment means months of interest never accrue on your balance — that is where the savings come from.
Why might the APR move up, not down? APR measures cost per rupee borrowed across the life of the loan. When you compress repayment into a shorter window, your fixed costs — processing fees, money-transfer charges — get spread over fewer months and a higher EMI, which can nudge APR up. In return, you cut tens of lakhs in interest you would otherwise have paid. The trade-off is almost always worth it in absolute rupee terms, even if the APR percentage moves in the wrong direction. Always compare loan options by total rupees paid, not APR alone.

What is APR and why does it matter?

APR is the total cost of borrowing money, expressed as a yearly percentage. It includes not just the interest rate but also other mandatory fees associated with the loan, such as processing fees, origination fees, and other charges.

APR gives you a clearer picture of the true cost of the loan. It allows you to compare different loan offers more accurately, as it considers all costs involved.

How to use this calculator: simply input your estimated loan amount, interest rate, and loan term, along with the other requested information. The calculator will then estimate your monthly EMI, total interest paid, the overall amount you will repay, and the APR percentage.

Understanding your repayment options

Most lenders offer four ways to handle payments while you are still in school. Each trades off how much you pay now against how much the loan costs in total. Remember: no lender offers a free period — interest you do not pay is added to what you owe.

Why currency matters

Your loan is in rupees, but your school is paid in dollars. Two separate currency costs raise your real APR above the advertised rate.

1. Rupee depreciation. The Indian Rupee has weakened against the US Dollar by roughly 4.0% to 4.5% per year on average over the last five years, driven by structural trade deficits and the inflation gap between the two economies. Each later semester's fees therefore cost more rupees than the first, so you borrow more.

2. Money-transfer charges. Every time money crosses the border there is a markup of roughly ₹0.50 to ₹1.00 per dollar over the spot rate. This applies when your loan's rupees are converted to pay the school, and again later if you earn in dollars and send money home to pay your EMIs.
